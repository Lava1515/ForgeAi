# Code-built direction

Project-authored guidance for the direction step. It restates, for this pipeline, what the installed skills `shorts-video-director`, `video-shot-planner`, `cinematic-camera`, `environment-artist`, `set-dressing` and `3d-animation-director` teach, and what the shot lists of the hand-made shorts did. The JSON shape and the limits in the prompt always win over this text.

## What a good shot list looks like

The shot list of the Earth-stops short, the one the owner liked: a spinning globe with "WHAT IF?" that jolts to a halt on the word "stopped"; a calm toy street seen wide from above, then at street level with a speed readout counting up; a close shot of one man with a coffee and equal arrows on him, a car and a lamp post; the same framing when the ground stops and only the loose things keep their arrows and launch; a wide side view of people and cars streaming east; street level into a wall of dust; a wave taller than the buildings; a bright pole with a penguin who shrugs. Three places (space, the street, the pole), eight shots, one man to follow, and in every shot something happens.

## Places

- 2 to 5 places, each a complete location: ground, something behind the action all the way to a horizon or far wall, 4 to 8 props around the subject, one landmark that makes the place recognisable, a sky or ceiling, a time of day.
- Layer every place in depth: the action zone in front, supporting props in the middle, simple silhouettes far away. Leave the middle of the frame free for the subject.
- Return to a place with a new camera and a new action rather than inventing another place.
- One palette for the whole video: 3 to 5 colours plus neutrals. The subject is the most saturated thing in the frame; backgrounds are calmer.
- A place must belong to the topic. A ready set is used only for what it really is.

## Shots

- One idea per shot. The picture shows the thing the narration is naming at that moment, not a general illustration of the topic.
- Each shot has one centre of attention. Say what it is and how large: the subject fills a third to two thirds of the frame height and sits in the middle 60 percent of the frame. Captions cover a band low in the frame, so the subject's important part stays above it.
- Give every shot a before and an after: something is different at the end of the shot than at its start (it arrived, grew, broke, lit up, fell, turned, a number counted up).
- Something moves from the first frame of a shot. A still object with a drifting camera is the weakest shot there is.
- The view changes every 2 to 4 seconds. A shot longer than 4 seconds gets a second beat: the camera changes framing or the action enters a new phase.
- Alternate framings between neighbours: wide then close, high then low, front then side. Never two wides in a row.
- On a map video, something new lands on the picture with every spoken phrase: a country fill, a label, a route segment, a marker, or a zoom to the next place the narration names. Write in each shot what lands on which phrase. No shot holds an unchanged picture for more than about 3 seconds; a held map on which new fills and labels keep landing counts as change and is often the best shot.
- The first shot shows the question inside the first second (the subject already on screen at frame 0, no fade from black, no empty establishing shot).
- The last shot is a finished picture: the pay-off or a return to the opening image.
- Follow one character or object through the video when the story has one, and keep it looking the same.

## Camera vocabulary

Name the framing (close, medium, wide) and one move per beat:

- push in: the camera travels toward the subject to raise attention on it.
- pull back: travels away to reveal where the subject is or how small it is.
- orbit: travels around the subject at a fixed distance; use an arc of 30 to 90 degrees, not a full circle.
- crane up or down: rises or sinks while aiming at the subject; a crane up from ground level to a high wide is a strong reveal.
- track: travels sideways alongside a moving subject.
- follow: stays behind or beside a moving subject at a fixed offset.
- tilt or pan: turns in place to move attention from one thing to another.
- reveal: starts tight and low on a detail, then swings out to show the whole.
- whip: a fast turn or travel that ends a beat; use it once or twice in a video.
- hold: a locked camera, right when the action itself is violent or fast.

A subject that moves or explodes needs a wider framing than one that stands still. Keep the camera outside solid things (walls, floors, buildings, planets) and inside the place's sky for the whole move, not only at its ends. Moves ease in and out; only a whip or an impact is abrupt.

## Readouts

- A readout is a number or 1 to 3 words that the narration is saying at that moment ("1,670 KM/H", "MYTH", "42 MINUTES"). It appears on the word, never earlier.
- Do not repeat the caption: the captions already show every spoken word. Most shots need no readout; 2 to 4 in a whole video is typical.

## What to avoid

- A place that is only a floor and a wall, or a subject floating in an empty colour.
- A subject too small to read on a phone.
- Abstract diagrams (arrows and rings around a ball) for more than one shot in a row: come back to a person, an animal or a place.
- Facial acting in close-up: the figures have simple faces. Tell emotion with the body (arms up, a jump, a slump, a shrug, running away).
- Text-heavy shots, tables, lists.

## Premise at the opening and ending

The subject and the premise text are on screen in the first frame. No logo or title card. End on the payoff and stop. No call to action.
